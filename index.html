Attention Is All You Need

Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser & Polosukhin (Google Brain / Google Research) — 2017 NeurIPS 2017 arXiv:1706.03762

The problem

The dominant sequence-transduction models in 2017 were recurrent (LSTM/GRU) encoder–decoders, optionally with attention bolted on. Recurrence is inherently sequential: hidden state hₜ depends on hₜ₋₁, so you cannot parallelise across the time dimension within a training example. This caps throughput and makes long-range dependencies hard to learn because the signal must traverse O(n) sequential steps.

The contribution

The Transformer dispenses with recurrence and convolution entirely, relying only on attention. The core building blocks:

  • Scaled dot-product attention: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ)V. The √dₖ scaling keeps dot products out of the softmax saturation region as dimension grows.
  • Multi-head attention: run h=8 attention functions in parallel on learned projections of Q, K, V, then concatenate. Each head can attend to a different relationship (syntax, coreference, position).
  • Positional encodings: since attention is permutation-invariant, fixed sinusoidal signals are added to inputs to inject order.
  • Encoder–decoder stack: N=6 identical layers each side, with residual connections, layer norm, and position-wise feed-forward sub-layers.

Crucially, every position attends to every other position in one parallel operation — O(1) sequential path length — making training massively parallel on GPUs/TPUs.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

Every model you deploy — GPT-4, Llama, Claude, BGE, the reranker in your RAG stack — is a Transformer. The O(n²) attention cost is the reason context windows are expensive and the target of every efficiency trick you will ever tune (FlashAttention, KV-cache, GQA, sliding-window, sparse attention). You cannot reason about latency, memory, or context limits without internalising this paper.

The insight

The title was a provocation that turned out to be literally true. Removing recurrence did not just speed up training — it made scale itself tractable. Every subsequent paper in this deck exists because the Transformer turned "train a bigger model" from an O(n)-sequential nightmare into an embarrassingly parallel matmul problem.

02

BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers

Devlin, Chang, Lee & Toutanova (Google AI Language) — 2018 NAACL 2019 arXiv:1810.04805

The problem

Earlier pretrained language models were unidirectional (GPT-1 predicted the next token left-to-right, so each token's representation could only see prior context) or only shallowly bidirectional (ELMo concatenated separately trained left-to-right and right-to-left models). For understanding tasks — classification, NER, span extraction — this is a handicap: the meaning of a word depends on tokens to its right as much as its left.

The contribution

BERT is an encoder-only Transformer pretrained with two self-supervised objectives that allow deep, jointly-bidirectional context:

  • Masked Language Modelling (MLM): randomly mask 15% of input tokens and predict them from both directions. Because the target is hidden, the model can attend left and right without trivially "seeing" the answer — the trick that unlocks bidirectionality.
  • Next-Sentence Prediction (NSP): given two segments, predict whether B actually follows A, teaching inter-sentence relationships (useful for QA / NLI).

The headline result was the pretrain → fine-tune paradigm: pretrain once on unlabelled text (BooksCorpus + Wikipedia), then fine-tune the same weights with one extra output layer for each downstream task. BERT-large (340M params) set state-of-the-art on 11 NLP tasks at once.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

BERT and its descendants (RoBERTa, DeBERTa, and the entire sentence-transformers family) are still the workhorses of retrieval and reranking. The embedding models and cross-encoders in your RAG pipeline are BERT-lineage encoders. When a model card says "based on BERT" or "mean-pooled MLM encoder", this is the paper that defines what that means. It also established the mental model — pretrain broad, adapt narrow — that still governs how you think about fine-tuning vs prompting.

The insight

BERT split the world into two architectural lineages that persist today: encoder-only models optimised for understanding (embeddings, classification) and decoder-only models optimised for generation (GPT-style). Knowing which family a model belongs to tells you instantly what it is good at — and BERT's MLM objective is precisely why encoders make better embeddings than raw decoders.

Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)

Brown, Mann, Ryder, Subbiah et al. (OpenAI) — 2020 NeurIPS 2020 arXiv:2005.14165

The problem

The BERT paradigm required fine-tuning on thousands of labelled examples per task, with a separate set of weights for each. Humans, by contrast, perform a new language task from a short instruction and a handful of examples. Could a sufficiently large language model do the same with no gradient updates?

The contribution

GPT-3 is a 175-billion-parameter decoder-only autoregressive Transformer — 10× larger than any prior dense model — trained to predict the next token on ~300B tokens of filtered Common Crawl, WebText, books and Wikipedia. The key empirical discovery is in-context learning: you can steer the model purely through the prompt.

  • Zero-shot: a task description only.
  • One-shot: description + one worked example.
  • Few-shot: description + K (typically 10–100) examples in the context window.

No weights change — the "learning" happens entirely in the forward pass, conditioned on the prompt. Few-shot performance on many benchmarks approached fine-tuned models, and improved smoothly with model size.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

This paper invented the job of prompt engineering and is the direct ancestor of every few-shot prompt, system message, and tool-use schema you write today. It reframed adaptation from "collect data and fine-tune" to "write a good prompt" — the default first move for any new task. It also gave us the now-ubiquitous decoder-only autoregressive architecture that GPT-4, Llama, Mistral and Claude all share.

The insight

In-context learning was an emergent capability — it was not trained for, it appeared as a side-effect of scale. That single observation reframed the field: instead of designing clever objectives, just make the model bigger and new abilities fall out. The natural next question — exactly how much does scale buy, and how do we spend it? — is answered by the next two papers.

04

Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan, Brown et al. (OpenAI) — 2020 arXiv-only arXiv:2001.08361

The problem

GPT-style results showed that bigger is better, but model design was still guesswork. How does test loss actually depend on model size, dataset size and compute? Is there structure you can extrapolate from cheap small runs to predict an expensive large one?

The contribution

Kaplan et al. showed that cross-entropy test loss follows smooth power laws across more than seven orders of magnitude in each of three quantities, when not bottlenecked by the others:

  • Model parameters N: L(N) ≈ (N₀/N)αN
  • Dataset size D: L(D) ≈ (D₀/D)αD
  • Compute C: L(C) ≈ (C₀/C)αC

Architecture details (width vs depth, attention heads) matter remarkably little compared to scale. Larger models are more sample-efficient — they reach a given loss with fewer tokens — so Kaplan concluded that for a fixed compute budget you should train very large models and stop well before convergence. (Slide 05 shows this conclusion was partly wrong.)

Why it matters to a practising engineer

Scaling laws turned LLM development into an engineering discipline with a budget. They let you run small pilots and predict the loss of a model you cannot yet afford to train, choose model size before committing GPU-months, and reason about whether a project is worth the compute. The same power-law thinking underlies inference-cost modelling and the decision of when more data or more parameters will (and will not) help.

The insight

Predictability is the real product here. Once you know loss is a power law, you stop guessing and start budgeting: a few cheap runs forecast the expensive one. The catch — corrected next slide — is that Kaplan held dataset size roughly fixed and so over-weighted parameters and under-weighted tokens.

05

Training Compute-Optimal LLMs (Chinchilla)

Hoffmann, Borgeaud, Mensch, Buchatskaya et al. (DeepMind) — 2022 NeurIPS 2022 arXiv:2203.15556

The problem

Following Kaplan, the industry built ever-larger models (GPT-3 175B, Gopher 280B, Megatron-Turing 530B) trained on roughly fixed ~300B-token budgets. DeepMind asked the precise question: for a fixed compute budget C, what is the optimal split between model size N and training tokens D?

The contribution

By training over 400 models across sizes and token counts and fitting the loss surface, Hoffmann et al. found that N and D should scale in roughly equal proportion — double the compute, double both parameters and tokens. The rule of thumb: about ~20 training tokens per parameter for compute-optimality.

This implied that GPT-3, Gopher and peers were badly undertrained — far too big for the data they saw. The proof: Chinchilla (70B params, 1.4T tokens) uses the same compute as Gopher (280B params, 300B tokens) yet outperforms it across the board (MMLU, BIG-bench, reading comprehension), while being 4× smaller and far cheaper to run at inference.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

Chinchilla reset how the whole field allocates compute and explains the shape of the models you actually use. Llama-class models are deliberately "over-trained" past the Chinchilla point because the optimum is for training compute — when you also weigh inference cost, a smaller model trained on more tokens is far cheaper to serve. When choosing between a 70B and a 7B model, this paper is why the smaller, data-rich model is so often the right call.

The insight

The most influential correction in modern LLM practice: most early giant models were undertrained, not too small. "Compute-optimal" is not the same as "deployment-optimal" — for anything you serve at scale, push tokens up and parameters down past the Chinchilla line, because you pay for inference forever and training only once.

06

What to Take Away

  • The Transformer is the substrate. Self-attention replaced recurrence, made training embarrassingly parallel, and is the architecture under every model you will ever deploy. Its O(n²) cost is the root of every context-window and latency trade-off.
  • Pretraining decoupled learning language from learning tasks. BERT's MLM established pretrain → fine-tune and the encoder lineage that still powers embeddings and rerankers.
  • Scale produces emergent abilities. GPT-3 showed in-context (zero/one/few-shot) learning with no gradient updates — the origin of prompt engineering and the decoder-only generation stack.
  • Scaling is predictable. Kaplan's power laws turned model design into budgeting: forecast the expensive run from cheap ones.
  • Compute allocation matters as much as compute. Chinchilla's ~20-tokens-per-parameter rule showed most early models were undertrained — and that inference cost should push you to smaller, data-richer models.
  • The through-line: attention made scale tractable → pretraining made it useful → few-shot revealed what scale unlocks → scaling laws made it predictable → Chinchilla made it efficient.
Where to next

These five papers get you a capable but unaligned base model — one that predicts plausible text, not one that follows instructions or behaves safely.
